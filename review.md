# ROLE AND OBJECTIVE

Act as a multidisciplinary curriculum review team responsible for auditing, improving, standardizing, and documenting an existing K-6 Catholic C-STREAM curriculum.

C-STREAM means:

**Catholic + Science + Technology + Religion/Faith Integration + Engineering + Art + Mathematics**

For purposes of this project, the "C" is not simply an opening prayer added to an otherwise secular STEM lesson. Catholic identity should meaningfully inform how students understand creation, human dignity, stewardship, truth, reason, creativity, ethical use of technology, service to others, vocation, community, and our responsibility to use our gifts well.

This curriculum is intended for a small Catholic elementary school in Minnesota within the **Archdiocese of Saint Paul and Minneapolis**.

The final curriculum must be:

- academically credible
- authentically Catholic
- developmentally appropriate for Kindergarten through Grade 6
- exceptionally easy for teachers to use
- usable by a substitute teacher with little or no C-STREAM experience
- hands-on
- inexpensive
- practical for a small school
- minimally dependent on computers and iPads
- measurable against clear standards
- engaging and fun
- realistic for Minnesota weather and seasons
- scalable to different class sizes
- sustainable year after year

Do not simply critique the curriculum. Your job is to help turn the repository into a coherent, defensible, classroom-ready K-6 C-STREAM program.

# 4. CREATE A K-6 LEARNING PROGRESSION

Build an intentional progression rather than seven independent collections of projects.

At minimum, evaluate progression across:

- K
- Grade 1
- Grade 2
- Grade 3
- Grade 4
- Grade 5
- Grade 6

It can also be useful to analyze developmental bands:

- K-1
- 2-3
- 4-6

Students should encounter recurring C-STREAM concepts with increasing sophistication.

Example:

Kindergarten:
"I built something."

Grade 1:
"I built something that solves a problem."

Grade 2:
"I can explain why I chose this design."

Grade 3:
"I can test my design."

Grade 4:
"I can use measurements and evidence to improve it."

Grade 5:
"I can compare designs using criteria and constraints."

Grade 6:
"I can defend my design choices using evidence, tradeoffs, cost, ethics, and performance."

Apply this philosophy throughout the curriculum.

Create a progression/matrix showing which major competencies students should have mastered or encountered by the end of each grade.

# 7. MAKE EVERY LESSON SUBSTITUTE-PROOF

This is one of the highest-priority requirements.

Assume occasionally that the person teaching the lesson:

- has never taught STEM
- has never taught engineering
- does not know the topic
- has never seen the materials
- was handed the lesson shortly before class
- may not be comfortable with technology

A substitute should NEVER need hidden institutional knowledge to make a lesson succeed.

Every lesson should have a standardized teacher-facing format.

Include:

## LESSON AT A GLANCE

- Grade
- Unit
- Lesson title
- Time required
- C-STREAM areas addressed
- Big idea
- Student-friendly learning objective
- Why students are learning this
- Catholic connection
- Standards
- Technology requirement
- Difficulty
- Prep time
- Cleanup time

## BEFORE CLASS

Exactly what the teacher should do.

Examples:

1. Put students into groups of 3.
2. Place one materials tray on each table.
3. Fill each cup with approximately 100 mL of water.
4. Write the question on the board.
5. Do NOT distribute the balloons until Step 4.

Use explicit instructions.

## MATERIALS

Include exact quantities where practical.

Separate:

- per student
- per group
- whole class
- teacher
- optional

## VOCABULARY

Give teacher-friendly definitions.

For K-1, vocabulary should be teachable orally and visually.

## TEACHER BACKGROUND

Explain what an adult who knows nothing about the topic needs to know.

Keep this concise but sufficient.

Include:

**Common misconception:**  
...

**If a student asks...:**  
...

**The important concept is...:**  
...

## EXACT LESSON SEQUENCE

Use numbered steps.

Include approximate times.

For example:

0-5 min: Hook  
5-10 min: Demonstration  
10-15 min: Explain challenge  
15-35 min: Build/test  
35-43 min: Discuss  
43-45 min: Cleanup/exit check

Adapt timing to existing class periods if the repository defines them.

Otherwise design lessons to be adaptable to 30-, 45-, and 60-minute periods where reasonable.

## QUESTIONS TO ASK STUDENTS

Supply actual questions.

Do not write only "facilitate discussion."

Provide questions such as:

- What do you notice?
- What changed?
- What stayed the same?
- Why do you think that happened?
- What evidence do you have?
- What could you change?
- How would you test that?
- Which solution worked best?
- What tradeoff did you make?
- How are we being good stewards of the materials we were given?

## WHAT SUCCESS LOOKS LIKE

Teachers need to know what evidence of learning to watch for.

## IF THINGS GO WRONG

Provide troubleshooting.

Example:

- If the motor doesn't spin...
- If the structure immediately falls...
- If students finish early...
- If the experiment produces no visible result...
- If there are not enough supplies...
- If the internet is unavailable...
- If devices are unavailable...

## CLEANUP

Give explicit cleanup instructions.

# 11. HOMEWORK PHILOSOPHY

The default is:

**No routine C-STREAM homework.**

Most learning should occur through guided classroom experiences.

Homework should only be proposed when it has a compelling reason.

Appropriate examples might include:

- observing the moon
- recording weather
- asking a family member about how something works
- locating examples of simple machines at home
- bringing a safe recyclable item for a future project
- interviewing someone about their work or vocation

Homework should:

- be short
- be meaningful
- not require parents to teach the lesson
- not require expensive materials
- not require a computer unless essential
- not create inequity between households

Whenever a lesson contains homework, explicitly state why it cannot reasonably be accomplished during class.

# 23. AUDIT EACH LESSON

For each existing lesson assign a status:

**KEEP**  
Strong as written.

**KEEP + IMPROVE**  
Concept is good but instructions, alignment, differentiation, faith integration, assessment, cost, or teacher usability needs improvement.

**REBUILD**  
Learning objective is valuable but lesson design is weak.

**REPLACE**  
A better activity can meet the objective.

**REMOVE**

# 32. BE WILLING TO DISAGREE WITH THE CURRENT CURRICULUM

Do not merely validate what already exists.

If something is weak, say so.

If a lesson is fun but educationally empty, identify that.

If a project is too complicated for its learning value, simplify it.

If an iPad activity would be better with cardboard and markers, recommend cardboard and markers.

If a $300 robot can be replaced by a $15 activity that teaches the concept better, recommend the $15 solution.

If an inexpensive physical activity should later transition to robotics because the robotics genuinely extends the learning, recommend that too.

The objective is not maximum technology.

The objective is excellent learning.

# 33. OPTIMIZE FOR THE TEACHER WHO ACTUALLY HAS TO TEACH IT

At every point ask:

> Could a capable elementary teacher who is NOT an expert in this subject understand this lesson, gather the materials, walk into the room, and teach it successfully?

Then ask:

> Could a substitute teacher do it?

Then ask:

> Would the students understand what they are learning rather than simply completing an activity?

Then ask:

> Could the teacher tell afterward whether the students actually learned it?

If the answer to any of these is no, improve the lesson.

# 34. OPTIMIZE FOR CHILDREN

The curriculum should produce classrooms where children regularly:

- ask questions
- touch things
- build things
- measure things
- test things
- draw things
- take things apart when appropriate
- solve problems
- make predictions
- make mistakes
- try again
- explain what happened
- collaborate
- occasionally work independently
- wonder
- create

Do not confuse quiet students completing worksheets with successful learning.

# 35. CATHOLIC C-STREAM NORTH STAR

Use this as the ultimate design test:

> We want students to understand God's creation more deeply, develop the reason and skills to explore it, gain the creativity and confidence to build and improve things within it, recognize the dignity and needs of the people affected by what they create, and understand that their knowledge and talents can be used in service of God and others.

Academic rigor and Catholic identity should strengthen one another.

# FINAL TEST

Before considering the curriculum complete, perform these seven simulations:

### SIMULATION 1: SUBSTITUTE
A substitute arrives 20 minutes before Grade 3 C-STREAM.

Can the substitute successfully teach today's lesson?

### SIMULATION 2: KINDERGARTEN
A Kindergarten student cannot read the instructions.

Can the student still fully participate?

### SIMULATION 3: NO DEVICES
The iPads are unavailable.

Which lessons still work?

Could unnecessary dependencies be removed?

### SIMULATION 4: MINNESOTA JANUARY
It is -10°F outside.

Does the week's curriculum still work?

### SIMULATION 5: LARGE CLASS
Enrollment unexpectedly rises to 25.

Do materials, teams, movement, and instructions still work?

### SIMULATION 6: BUDGET
The school has limited funding.

Can we teach a high-quality program without purchasing expensive specialized equipment?

### SIMULATION 7: PARENT
A parent asks:

"What exactly is my child learning in C-STREAM?"

Can the teacher clearly explain the skills, standards, Catholic connection, and progression?

If any simulation exposes a weakness, improve the curriculum.

The finished product should feel like a deliberately engineered K-6 academic program, not a folder full of STEM activities.
