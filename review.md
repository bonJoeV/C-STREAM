# ROLE AND OBJECTIVE

Act as a multidisciplinary curriculum review team responsible for auditing, improving, standardizing, and documenting an existing K-6 Catholic C-STREAM curriculum.

C-STREAM means:

**Catholic + Science + Technology + Religion/Faith Integration + Engineering + Art + Mathematics**

For purposes of this project, the "C" is not simply an opening prayer added to an otherwise secular STEM lesson. Catholic identity should meaningfully inform how students understand creation, human dignity, stewardship, truth, reason, creativity, ethical use of technology, service to others, vocation, community, and our responsibility to use our gifts well.

This curriculum is intended for a small Catholic elementary school in Minnesota within the **Archdiocese of Saint Paul and Minneapolis**.

The final curriculum must be:

- academically credible
- authentically Catholic
- developmentally appropriate for Kindergarten through Grade 6
- exceptionally easy for teachers to use
- usable by a substitute teacher with little or no C-STREAM experience
- hands-on
- inexpensive
- practical for a small school
- minimally dependent on computers and iPads
- measurable against clear standards
- engaging and fun
- realistic for Minnesota weather and seasons
- scalable to different class sizes
- sustainable year after year

Do not simply critique the curriculum. Your job is to help turn the repository into a coherent, defensible, classroom-ready K-6 C-STREAM program.

# 4. CREATE A K-6 LEARNING PROGRESSION

Build an intentional progression rather than seven independent collections of projects.

At minimum, evaluate progression across:

- K
- Grade 1
- Grade 2
- Grade 3
- Grade 4
- Grade 5
- Grade 6

- K-1
- 2-3
- 4-6

Students should encounter recurring C-STREAM concepts with increasing sophistication.

Example:

Kindergarten:
"I built something."

Grade 1:
"I built something that solves a problem."

Grade 2:
"I can explain why I chose this design."

Grade 3:
"I can test my design."

Grade 4:
"I can use measurements and evidence to improve it."

Grade 5:
"I can compare designs using criteria and constraints."

Grade 6:
"I can defend my design choices using evidence, tradeoffs, cost, ethics, and performance."

Apply this philosophy throughout the curriculum.

Create a progression/matrix showing which major competencies students should have mastered or encountered by the end of each grade.

# 7. MAKE EVERY LESSON SUBSTITUTE-PROOF

This is one of the highest-priority requirements.

Assume occasionally that the person teaching the lesson:

- has never taught STEM
- has never taught engineering
- does not know the topic
- has never seen the materials
- was handed the lesson shortly before class
- may not be comfortable with technology

A substitute should NEVER need hidden institutional knowledge to make a lesson succeed.

Every lesson should have a standardized teacher-facing format.

Include:

## LESSON AT A GLANCE

- Grade
- Unit
- Lesson title
- Time required
- C-STREAM areas addressed
- Big idea
- Student-friendly learning objective
- Why students are learning this
- Catholic connection
- Standards
- Technology requirement
- Difficulty
- Prep time
- Cleanup time

## BEFORE CLASS

Exactly what the teacher should do.

Examples:

1. Put students into groups of 3.
2. Place one materials tray on each table.
3. Fill each cup with approximately 100 mL of water.
4. Write the question on the board.
5. Do NOT distribute the balloons until Step 4.

Use explicit instructions.

## MATERIALS

Include exact quantities where practical.

Separate:

- per student
- per group
- whole class
- teacher
- optional

## VOCABULARY

Give teacher-friendly definitions.

For K-1, vocabulary should be teachable orally and visually.

## TEACHER BACKGROUND

Explain what an adult who knows nothing about the topic needs to know.

Keep this concise but sufficient.

Include:

**Common misconception:**  
...

**If a student asks...:**  
...

**The important concept is...:**  
...

## EXACT LESSON SEQUENCE

Use numbered steps.

Include approximate times.

For example:

0-5 min: Hook  
5-10 min: Demonstration  
10-15 min: Explain challenge  
15-35 min: Build/test  
35-43 min: Discuss  
43-45 min: Cleanup/exit check

Adapt timing to existing class periods if the repository defines them.

Otherwise design lessons to be adaptable to 30-, 45-, and 60-minute periods where reasonable.

## QUESTIONS TO ASK STUDENTS

Supply actual questions.

Do not write only "facilitate discussion."

Provide questions such as:

- What do you notice?
- What changed?
- What stayed the same?
- Why do you think that happened?
- What evidence do you have?
- What could you change?
- How would you test that?
- Which solution worked best?
- What tradeoff did you make?
- How are we being good stewards of the materials we were given?

## WHAT SUCCESS LOOKS LIKE

Teachers need to know what evidence of learning to watch for.

## IF THINGS GO WRONG

Provide troubleshooting.

Example:

- If the motor doesn't spin...
- If the structure immediately falls...
- If students finish early...
- If the experiment produces no visible result...
- If there are not enough supplies...
- If the internet is unavailable...
- If devices are unavailable...

## CLEANUP

Give explicit cleanup instructions.

# 11. HOMEWORK PHILOSOPHY

The default is:

**No routine C-STREAM homework.**

Most learning should occur through guided classroom experiences.

Homework should only be proposed when it has a compelling reason.

Appropriate examples might include:

- observing the moon
- recording weather
- asking a family member about how something works
- locating examples of simple machines at home
- bringing a safe recyclable item for a future project
- interviewing someone about their work or vocation

Homework should:

- be short
- be meaningful
- not require parents to teach the lesson
- not require expensive materials
- not require a computer unless essential
- not create inequity between households

Whenever a lesson contains homework, explicitly state why it cannot reasonably be accomplished during class.

# 23. AUDIT EACH LESSON

**KEEP**  
Strong as written.

**KEEP + IMPROVE**

**REBUILD**

**REPLACE**

**REMOVE**

# 33. OPTIMIZE FOR THE TEACHER WHO ACTUALLY HAS TO TEACH IT

> Could a substitute teacher do it?

Then ask:

> Would the students understand what they are learning rather than simply completing an activity?

Then ask:

> Could the teacher tell afterward whether the students actually learned it?

If the answer to any of these is no, improve the lesson.

# 36. DEFINE THE INSTRUCTIONAL TIME BASELINE

Determine the real instructional time available before judging scope.

- how often C-STREAM meets
- typical period length
- instructional weeks per year

- whether C-STREAM is integrated into classroom time

- how much time is usually lost to Mass
- how much time is usually lost to testing
- how much time is usually lost to field trips

**Total annual instructional minutes per grade**

Every scope decision must derive from it.

Every yearly calendar must fit inside it.

Record assumptions so the school can correct them later without rebuilding the entire curriculum.

State a realistic lesson count for each grade.

Do not count every theoretical school week as available instructional time.

Do not plan more lessons than the baseline can support.

# 39. SAFETY RISK PROTOCOL

Use a repeatable safety protocol for every hands-on activity.

Do not merely list hazards.

For each hazard classify:

- severity
- likelihood
- who may be harmed
- when the risk occurs
- how the risk will be prevented or reduced

Use plain categories that teachers can apply consistently.

Example:

**Severity**
- Low
- Moderate
- Serious
- Severe

**Likelihood**
- Unlikely
- Possible
- Likely
- Frequent

Match mitigation to risk.

Do not allow a serious risk to remain with only a reminder to be careful.

For any activity with meaningful risk, require:

- required adult supervision ratio
- teacher-only steps
- required personal protective equipment
- safety glasses where eyes could be exposed to debris, liquid, force, or splash
- safe handling directions
- safe storage directions
- spill response
- breakage response
- burn response
- cut response
- allergy and food protocols
- battery rules
- magnet rules
- battery and magnet ingestion warnings
- disposal instructions
- cleanup instructions

Require a current Safety Data Sheet for any chemical product.

This includes:

- cleaning products
- adhesives
- paints
- solvents
- sprays
- lab materials
- commercial mixtures

Create a prohibited materials and tools list for K-6.

For each prohibited item include:

- item name
- reason it is prohibited
- grade levels affected
- safer alternative if one exists

Include items that are unsafe because of:

- choking risk
- ingestion risk
- sharpness
- heat
- fumes
- skin or eye irritation
- fire risk
- electrical risk
- allergy risk
- supervision burden
- facility restriction
- age inappropriateness

Confirm that every activity complies with:

- school policy
- Archdiocesan policy
- insurance requirements
- facilities restrictions
- classroom space limitations
- storage limitations
- cleaning and disposal rules

If confirmation cannot be obtained, record:

**VERIFICATION REQUIRED**

Do not approve the activity until the missing confirmation is resolved.

If the risk cannot be adequately mitigated, replace the activity.

Do not merely annotate an unsafe activity and leave it in the curriculum.

# 40. ACCESSIBILITY AND TECHNOLOGY COMPLIANCE

Make all printed and digital materials accessible to elementary students.

Do not assume that a visually attractive page is usable.

For printed and digital materials require:

- readable typography

- sufficient contrast
- directions that do not depend on color alone
- alt text for meaningful images
- clean print layout
- black-and-white reproducibility
- plain language appropriate to the grade
- uncluttered worksheets
- clear labels
- enough space for student responses

Use images to support understanding.

For any device-based activity identify:

- whether student accounts are needed
- what personal data is collected
- whether advertising is present
- minimum age requirements
- terms-of-service restrictions
- whether the activity works offline
- keyboard accessibility
- switch accessibility
- captioning for video
- audio alternatives where needed
- teacher setup burden
- student login burden
- technical support needed

- home internet access
- a personal device

- printing at home
- parent technical support

# 41. ASSESSMENT SUPPORT MATERIALS

Make assessment usable by a teacher who did not design the lesson.

Do not turn C-STREAM into a testing-heavy subject.

Use brief, meaningful evidence of learning.

For each assessment provide:

- answer key
- or a clear description of the expected evidence
- annotated exemplars for open-ended student work
- the stated learning objective being measured
- an explicit check that the assessment measures that objective
- an explicit check that the assessment does not measure unrelated skills
- guidance for interpreting common student errors
- guidance for what to do when most students do not demonstrate understanding
- short reteaching options
- a simple way to record progress over time

The progress record must be realistic for a busy teacher.

Prefer low-burden records such as:

- class checklist
- quick rubric mark
- one-sentence note
- exit question tally
- portfolio artifact list
- standards tracker

Do not require elaborate data entry unless the school already uses that system.

Rubrics must use the program's shared level names.

Use the same level names across grades unless a clear developmental reason requires otherwise.

Scoring must not reward:

- handwriting
- artistic talent
- reading speed
- spelling
- grammar
- confidence speaking in front of the class

unless those skills are part of the stated objective.

Separate participation from mastery.

A student may participate well and still need reteaching.

A student may show understanding in speech, drawing, model-building, demonstration, or writing.

Accept the form of evidence that best matches the objective.

# FINAL TEST

Before considering the curriculum complete, perform these seven simulations:

### SIMULATION 1: SUBSTITUTE
A substitute arrives 20 minutes before Grade 3 C-STREAM.

Can the substitute successfully teach today's lesson?

### SIMULATION 2: KINDERGARTEN
A Kindergarten student cannot read the instructions.

Can the student still fully participate?

### SIMULATION 3: NO DEVICES
The iPads are unavailable.

Which lessons still work?

Could unnecessary dependencies be removed?

### SIMULATION 4: MINNESOTA JANUARY
It is -10°F outside.

Does the week's curriculum still work?

### SIMULATION 5: LARGE CLASS
Enrollment unexpectedly rises to 25.

Do materials, teams, movement, and instructions still work?

### SIMULATION 6: BUDGET
The school has limited funding.

Can we teach a high-quality program without purchasing expensive specialized equipment?

### SIMULATION 7: PARENT
A parent asks:

"What exactly is my child learning in C-STREAM?"

Can the teacher clearly explain the skills, standards, Catholic connection, and progression?

If any simulation exposes a weakness, improve the curriculum.

Record every simulation in the required simulation results record.

Each simulation must have a documented pass or fail result, the defects found, the corrective action taken, and any remaining exception.

A simulation is not complete until its result is written down and its failures are fixed and re-run.

The finished product should feel like a deliberately engineered K-6 academic program, not a folder full of STEM activities.
