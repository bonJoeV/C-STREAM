    Weeks 2-3: Flight Fundamentals¶

    Lesson at a glance¶

    Field Teacher information
    Grade / unit / title Grades 3-4 / Flight / Flight Fundamentals
    Time Two 40-minute meetings, cleanup included
    Domains Catholic honest inquiry, physical science, measurement
    Big idea Air exerts forces on a moving glider; evidence about one changed feature helps us improve it.
    Student objective "I can label forces and compare three flights before and after one change."
    Why Pilots and designers use careful tests, not one lucky result.
    Catholic connection Faith and reason support honest inquiry; give credit for another person's design and report inconvenient results. See the Vatican's Fides et Ratio, paraphrased, not a scientific proof of faith.
    Local standards Local, not official: CST-C1 (honest evidence); CST-S2 (one changed feature and repeated trials); CST-S3 (force model); CST-M1 (measure/compare).
    Technology None; teacher photos/video optional, not needed
    Difficulty / prerequisites Moderate; count and compare whole centimeters. No mean, percentage, formal force calculation or prior Year A required.
    Prep / cleanup 15 minutes plus obtaining an approved indoor flight area; 5 minutes each meeting

    Before class¶

    1. Arrange pairs, one trio for odd enrollment. Tape two parallel 3 m lanes, 1 m wide, with a launch line and stopping boundary. Obtain approval for hall use; block through-traffic. If unavailable, use the short-flight alternative below.
    2. Fold one sample as described below and make the nose blunt. Prepare a large paper plane outline and four arrow labels.
    3. Board: A: trials 1/2/3 | one change | B: trials 1/2/3. Have pupils copy tables instead of printing.
    4. Mark paper with team ID and A/B. Set storage tray for meeting 2.

    Exact supplies¶

    Tables budget ceil(students / 2) kits. A final trio gives 5/7/10/12 active teams at 10/15/20/25 pupils; unused eighth/thirteenth kit is spare. Thirteen-team timing is an upper capacity bound.

    • Per student: pencil, 1 evidence half-sheet each meeting.
    • Per team: 2 letter-paper sheets for gliders, 2 paper recording sheets across the unit, 1 ruler.
    • Whole class: 2 measuring tapes marked in centimeters, 6 m floor-safe tape for launch/boundary markers, timer, 1 storage tray.
    • Teacher only: 2 demonstration paper sheets, 4 paper force labels and board; scissors if trimming a damaged nose. No clips, hair dryers, hot-air devices or fans needed.

    Vocabulary, background, misconception and answer¶

    Weight: Earth's downward gravitational force. Lift: aerodynamic force roughly perpendicular to motion through the air. Drag: aerodynamic force opposing that motion. Thrust: propulsion force; the hand pushes a paper plane during launch, not after release. Trial: one repeated test.

    Wings change airflow and pressure; a curved upper surface is not required. Do not teach that split air must meet again at the back of a wing, or that lift must exceed weight in every climb. The force arrows here are a simplified model. A released glider has no engine thrust; gravity helps sustain its descending motion.

    Common misconception: "Paper planes fall only because they run out of thrust." They glide while aerodynamic forces and gravity act.

    If asked: "Why does flat paper fly?" Answer: "At an angle to moving air, a flat wing changes the air's motion and experiences lift. We can test its flight without claiming to measure every force."

    Accuracy source: NASA Glenn's Incorrect Airfoil Theory. Bartolomeu de Gusmao is not presented as a Blessed or saint; his biography and devotional status are VERIFICATION REQUIRED before a historical extension.

    SAFETY¶

    Only paper gliders with folded-back blunt noses. No clips, pins, hard weights, elastic launchers, hair dryers, standing on chairs or throwing toward anyone. Teacher gives launch/retrieve signals. Operators stand at the line; everyone else stays behind it. Retrieve only after both lanes stop. Stop testing when traffic enters the area. Do not go outdoors in ice, storms, unsafe wind or cold.

    Fold the baseline glider: teacher demonstration¶

    1. Place letter paper portrait; fold lengthwise, then open.
    2. Fold the two top corners to the center crease.
    3. Fold each new sloping top edge toward the center crease.
    4. Fold the whole plane closed along the center.
    5. Fold each side down to make matching wings; align the top wing edge with the lower body edge.
    6. Open wings to a shallow V, keeping them equal.
    7. Fold the nose back 1 cm; do not cut it to a point. Mark A.

    An adult may precrease for accessibility. Students still choose the test change.

    Meeting 1: forces and baseline, exactly 40 minutes¶

    1. 0-4: Wonder. Original prayer: "God, help us seek truth and learn patiently." Drop a flat paper sheet. "What pushes or pulls on it?"
    2. 4-10: Force model. Draw weight down, lift roughly up and drag opposing travel. Show the hand's launch push; remove the thrust arrow after release. Ask: "Does our paper plane have an engine?" "What is still acting after release?"
    3. 10-17: Fold together. Demonstrate the seven steps. Partners alternate folds; each child labels forces on a separate drawing.
    4. 17-21: Fair-test plan. Same paper, plane, launch spot, launch height and launcher for three trials. A gentle level push, not an overhead throw. Measure from line to first ground contact, not slide distance. Record whole centimeters; human throws still vary.
    5. 21-30: Three flights. Two lanes operate concurrently. Each of 13 teams takes at most a 1-minute slot (7 rounds across two lanes); reserve 2 minutes for resets. Teacher signals launch then retrieval. Partner measures; launcher remains the same for all A/B trials; rotate other roles so all children contribute.
    6. 30-35: Individual evidence. Copy three observed distances and circle shortest/longest. "Did every trial match? Why might they differ?" Grade 3 compares values; Grade 4 also calculates the whole-number range, largest minus smallest. No mean required.
    7. 35-40: Cleanup. Store labeled planes flat, return rulers/tapes, clear floor markings if the space must reopen, collect force drawings and tables.

    Meeting 2: one change and comparison, exactly 40 minutes¶

    1. 0-5: Retrieve. Review three A results. "Why isn't one flight enough?"
    2. 5-10: Choose one feature. Keep the same plane and fold both trailing wing edges up 5 mm using the ruler. Label B. Predict the effect; equal changes on both wings count as one feature, not a change in paper/launcher.
    3. 10-16: Build and rehearse. Teacher shows the fold and checks blunt noses. Record "upturned trailing edges" and a prediction. Do not promise that it improves distance.
    4. 16-27: Three B trials. Use the same launcher, height, lanes and first-contact measurement. Teacher runs two concurrent lanes, up to 7 one-minute rounds plus resets. Waiting teams annotate force diagrams.
    5. 27-32: Compare. Line up the A/B tables. Grade 3: compare shortest and longest, using whole-number differences if ready. Grade 4: compare ranges; explain overlap and why results do not prove one design always wins. Ask: "What evidence supports your recommendation? What would another trial tell us?"
    6. 32-35: Individual evidence. Each child names the changed feature and two kept-same features, identifies evidence for a next test, and explains that unexpected results must be kept.
    7. 35-40: Cleanup. Return instruments, recycle or store paper as local rules permit, remove trip hazards and collect slips. No required flight at home.

    Success, troubleshooting and differentiation¶

    Success: each child labels weight/lift/drag and identifies the launch push, records actual repeated distances, and distinguishes a change from a control. Score 1 assertion only; 2 diagram/data with prompts; 3 correct force model and fair comparison; 4 level 3 plus explains variation or model limitations. Teacher notes absent measurements rather than substituting invented data.

    • Nose dives: check equal wings/nose; log a repair before restarting trials. Repairs change conditions, so compare a new baseline.
    • Beyond 3 m: record at least 300 cm, shorten the gentle launch consistently for a new set. Do not fabricate a landing distance.
    • No approved long area / winter indoor alternative: launch from a seated desk into a 1 m clear tray-lined floor lane, with the teacher as launcher for all trials. Record the new lane cap and compare only within it. No corridor test amid foot traffic.
    • Support / Grade 3: precrease, read labels aloud, let children dictate counts and point to controls; use a tape with whole-centimeter marks.
    • Challenge / Grade 4: repeat one design, compare ranges, propose a better control for launch force. Do not infer lift magnitude from distance alone.
    • 25 pupils: thirteen teams, two approved lanes; at most seven one-minute rounds per test window. If only one safe lane exists, teacher tests a selected design while every child uses the explicitly labeled class dataset; personal plane performance is not assessed.

    Family snippet¶

    We modeled flight forces and tested one wing change using repeated measurements. Each child compared evidence, not just a favorite plane. Our Catholic connection was honest investigation and patience. Ask: "Which feature changed, and what stayed the same?" Optional at home: explain the force arrows using hands. No throwing, devices or homework required.
