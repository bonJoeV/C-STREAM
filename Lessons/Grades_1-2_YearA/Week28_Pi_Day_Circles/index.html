    Week 28: Pi Day Circles¶

    Lesson at a glance¶

    Field Teacher information
    Grade / unit / title Grades 1-2 / Mathematics and design / Pi Day Circles
    Time / domains One 30-minute meeting / C, M, A
    Big idea We can compare distances around circles using the same unit.
    Student objective "I can compare two circle edges and use circles to make a repeating design."
    Why Accurate measurement and purposeful patterns matter more than memorizing digits.
    Catholic connection Honest evidence: report approximate measures without pretending they are exact; teacher application of faith/reason, CCC 159 (source below).
    Standards Local CST-C1: name measurement uncertainty; CST-M1: compare two boundaries; CST-M2: extend a repeating pattern; CST-A2: intentional form/pattern. Official benchmarks: VERIFICATION REQUIRED.
    Technology None primary path: paper/string directly show the mathematics. Robot art is not required and attaching a marker to a rolling Sphero is not part of this lesson.
    Difficulty / prep / cleanup Low; 10 minutes; 4 minutes cleanup included.

    Before class / supplies¶

    1. Choose two smooth round lids about 8 cm and 12 cm across (neither wider than 15 cm), so their boundaries fit the 60 cm yarn. Remove sharp metal edges. Trace a square to compare.
    2. Cut 60 cm lengths of yarn; prepare one equal-size block chain per team. Demonstrate wrapping yarn once with no overlap or stretching.
    3. Draw AB and AAB circle patterns on the board. Do not use food rewards.
    4. Per student: one sheet and crayon.
    5. Per team of 2-3: two round lids, two yarn lengths, 20 equal blocks about 2 cm wide (or 20 equal 2 cm paper squares) and scrap paper for comparing laid-out yarn. Check that the unit chain spans the longer boundary before class.
    6. Class: tape and board, circle/square examples; reusable lids/yarn.
    7. Teacher-only: scissors for precutting yarn; optional ruler to show approximation.

    Vocabulary / teacher background¶

    Circle = flat round shape; circumference = distance around its boundary; diameter = distance across through the center; approximate = close, not exact; repeating unit = part that repeats. Pi is a finite number about 3.14, the ratio of circumference to diameter. Its decimal representation does not terminate or repeat. Endless digits do not mean infinite magnitude. No ratio calculation is required of Grades 1-2. Misconception: every round-looking object is a flat circle. A ball is a sphere; its flat outline may look circular. If asked "Is pi as big as God?" No. Mathematics describes a finite quantity; a devotional analogy is not a mathematical or theological measurement.

    Exact numbered sequence (30 minutes)¶

    1. 0-4: Find a circle versus square. Explain Pi Day's 3/14 date briefly without digit recitation or theology claims.
    2. 4-8: Model wrapping yarn around one lid, marking the join, laying it flat and comparing to the same equal blocks.
    3. 8-18: Team members each help measure both lids. Align yarn starts; predict then compare. Grade 2 counts equal blocks, including "a little more than" if needed; Grade 1 points to longer/shorter. Repeat once to notice small measurement differences.
    4. 18-23: Each child traces circles in an intentional AB (Grade 1) or AAB/ABB (Grade 2) pattern. Partner extends it and explains the repeating unit.
    5. 23-26: Individual exit: identify the longer boundary, explain one measurement step and point to the repeating unit; say why a measure may be approximate.
    6. 26-30: Cleanup.

    Questions / success¶

    Which yarn is longer when starts line up? What made our two attempts differ? What part of your pattern repeats? - Grade 1: correctly compare two boundaries; demonstrate lining up endpoints; create two complete AB units and predict the next circle. - Grade 2: record two approximate counts in the same units and justify comparison; create/extend three units of AAB or ABB; identify one uncertainty. - Teacher records not yet / prompted / independent for comparison, measurement method, pattern and honest uncertainty. No coding credit from circle artwork.

    If things go wrong / indoor fallback¶

    Slipping yarn: tape the starting end or teacher models a paper strip. No lids: use two teacher-drawn circles and paper strips; describe these as drawings. No equal blocks: use identical paper squares. Entire lesson works indoors without devices. Early finishers compare a third circle, not speed.

    SAFETY¶

    No food/treats, loose coins, sharp can lids, neck loops or strings tied to students. Precut yarn; teacher manages scissors. Accessible tabletop work only.

    Support / challenge / cleanup¶

    Support: pretraced circles, oral prompts and partner stabilization without taking the child's decision. Grade 1 responds by pointing/drawing. Challenge: Grade 2 compare around/across lengths informally; no required division. Cleanup: count reusable lids/blocks, coil yarn into bags, recycle scraps and retain each child's pattern.

    Family snippet / sources¶

    We compared circle boundaries with yarn and designed a repeating circle pattern. Catholic connection: honest measurement, including uncertainty. Ask: "How did you decide which circle was bigger around?" Optional: point out two circles in a room or picture; no materials/device required. No routine homework. Pi explanation gives definition and approximate value. CCC 159 concerns faith and science; the classroom honesty connection is an application, not an official benchmark. Ecclesiastes 3:11 may be read from a school-approved Bible; no unverified quotation is reproduced.
