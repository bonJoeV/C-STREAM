    K-6 C-STREAM learning progression¶

    Local planning framework, dated 2026-10-04 for 2026-27. Not MDE/Archdiocese-approved. Use the fixed meanings in Local Standards and external-version limits in Standards Sources.

    These are observable end-of-grade targets, not new external benchmarks and not evidence of existing curriculum coverage. Each grade increases the student's agency, precision, explanation and ability to consider others. Earlier skills remain in use. I/D targets guide formative assessment; M checkpoints require the individual evidence described below. Access supports are always allowed.

    For K-1, demonstrate and give short oral/picture instructions; accept pointing, acting, construction and dictated explanations. For 2-3, add labels, repeated tests and simple comparisons. For 4-6, add independent plans, evidence, limitations and justified tradeoffs, not merely larger projects.

    Catholic integration: reason, dignity and stewardship¶

    End of grade CST-C1: faith/reason and honest evidence CST-C2: dignity/service/inclusive ethical design CST-C3: stewardship/resources/work
    K Tell or show one thing actually observed; connect careful noticing with the taught idea of wonder at creation. Offer a classmate a usable turn/role and identify someone a simple design could help. Return a shared tool, sort a reusable piece and tell why materials need care.
    1 Distinguish an observation from a guess in a demonstration; report an unexpected result honestly. Describe a user's need and make one change that helps the user participate. Choose a reusable material and describe careful work as a way to care for gifts and people.
    2 Compare a prediction with a recorded result and explain why truthful results matter, even when disappointing. Explain how a design helps a person and identify a feature that might make it hard for another person to use. Compare two material choices for waste and function; carry out and explain a less-wasteful choice.
    3 Use recorded evidence to revise an explanation; explain in simple terms that study of creation and taught faith need not compete. Ask a user-centered question, adapt a feature and explain the adaptation without stereotyping the user. Keep a simple resource record and explain a reuse decision that protects function and respects others' work.
    4 Separate an evidence-based claim from a taught faith/ethical reflection; acknowledge a limitation and credit an idea. Compare solutions for access, safety and service; justify an inclusive choice using a realistic user scenario. Compare waste, durability and repair possibilities; defend a responsible choice under a material limit.
    5 Explain how a claim is supported or challenged by evidence; distinguish scientific explanation, uncertainty and Catholic ethical judgment. Identify a possible unintended harm or exclusion and propose an appropriate safeguard before testing. Evaluate material use and possible effects on people/creation; explain why skilled work and service matter beyond winning.
    6 Defend an honest, limited conclusion, correct an error and explain the taught compatibility of faith/reason without treating faith as test data. Defend a service-oriented design using evidence about user needs, accessibility and privacy/safety; identify a remaining ethical tradeoff. Recommend a resource/work plan considering durability, waste, repair and effects on people/creation; acknowledge a tradeoff.

    Evidence examples: Individual oral observation versus prediction (K-1); explanation of one inclusive/resource choice (2-3); annotated ethical design decision with evidence and a limit (4-6). Assess understanding and actions, not private belief or spiritual maturity.

    Science: inquiry, investigations and systems¶

    End of grade CST-S1: observations/questions/evidence CST-S2: investigations/testing/variables CST-S3: physical/life/Earth systems/models
    K Show a detail or change using senses safely; ask a question about what was noticed. Make a pictured/oral prediction, follow a teacher-set safe test and show the result. Make or describe a simple representation of an observed physical object, living thing or weather feature.
    1 Record two observations with pictures/marks and use them to answer a simple question. Compare two teacher-set conditions and identify what was different and what was observed. Identify important parts of a physical, living or Earth/space example and show a relationship using a model.
    2 Ask a testable question, record observations and explain an answer using at least one recorded result. With a scaffold, change one feature, keep another consistent and repeat a test to compare results. Use a labelled model to explain a simple change in a physical, life or Earth/space system.
    3 Use repeated observations or simple data to describe a pattern and explain how it answers the question. Identify the changed feature, measured outcome and a feature kept consistent; carry out repeated safe trials. Describe interacting parts and cause/effect in models across physical, life and Earth/space contexts.
    4 Support a claim with recorded results and distinguish the result from an explanation or new question. Plan a fair comparison, record repeated measurements and explain why a controlled feature matters. Use a model to predict a system change and compare that prediction with observation in each science context.
    5 Compare evidence from more than one trial/source, notice disagreement and propose a useful follow-up question. Identify changed, measured and controlled variables; describe a repeatable method and one source of uncertainty. Explain a physical, life or Earth/space interaction with a model and identify an important feature the model omits.
    6 Defend a claim with relevant data, address conflicting evidence and state what the investigation cannot establish. Conduct and explain a safe, repeatable controlled investigation; discuss variation and a justified improvement to the method. Use and revise models in physical, life and Earth/space contexts to explain/predict change, identify a limit and compare with evidence.

    Evidence examples: Observation sketch and explanation (K-1); individual description of changed/consistent features and trial record (2-3); investigation plan, evidence-supported claim and model limitation (4-6). Across the year, collect physical, life and Earth/space examples for CST-S3; one context is insufficient. A craft labelled "science" is not a scientific explanation.

    Technology and computing: tools, algorithms and impacts¶

    End of grade CST-T1: tools/input-output/troubleshooting CST-T2: algorithms/loops/conditions/debugging CST-T3: data/digital citizenship/technology impacts
    K Select and safely demonstrate a tool for a task; show what action causes an observable result. Put at least three pictured actions in order, follow them and locate one misplaced action with support. Sort objects by an observable feature and identify, in a scenario, information to keep private and an adult to ask for help.
    1 Explain a tool's job and describe an action/input and result/output; check an obvious problem safely. Give a precise sequence, identify a repeated action and correct a step that produces the wrong result. Record a simple sort/count and explain a safe, kind choice in a teacher-provided technology scenario.
    2 Trace input, action and output in a simple system; describe a problem and test one likely cause. Represent a sequence and repeated group of steps; follow a simple if/then choice and correct an error. Explain a simple data display, distinguish private from shareable information and acknowledge another person's idea.
    3 Compare tools for a purpose and trace a system's input/output; document a check rather than changing parts at random. Write/arrange instructions with a loop and condition, trace two possible cases and explain a correction. Collect anonymous task data, explain what it shows and choose responsible sharing/credit in a realistic scenario.
    4 Break a system problem into parts and test causes in order; explain how each check narrows the problem. Decompose a task, represent loops and conditions, and use a trace to locate and repair an error. Identify missing/misleading data and explain a technology benefit, possible harm and protective action.
    5 Choose a tool/system for function and access; document input/output checks and a justified troubleshooting sequence. Compare algorithms for the same task, test contrasting inputs and revise for correctness and clarity. Evaluate data relevance and collection limits; explain privacy, credit and access decisions in a technology use case.
    6 Defend a safe tool/system choice and use documented checks to distinguish likely causes from unsupported guesses. Design and explain a decomposed algorithm with repetition and conditions; test usual and boundary cases and justify debugging revisions. Evaluate a data-informed technology proposal for accuracy, privacy, accessibility and social effects; justify safeguards and a tradeoff.

    Evidence examples: Individual tool explanation and movement-card trace (K-1); loop/choice demonstration and privacy scenario (2-3); algorithm trace, troubleshooting log or technology-impact recommendation (4-6). Local algorithms can be unplugged. Claim actual device programming only after individually checking executable work on a device; cards alone do not establish it.

    Engineering: requirements, iteration and decisions¶

    End of grade CST-E1: problems/criteria/constraints CST-E2: build/test/redesign CST-E3: tradeoffs/communicate solutions
    K Name a problem and show what a solution should do using a teacher-given material limit. Build a simple model, try it and identify/change a part that did not work. Show a design choice and give a reason using words, pointing or a demonstration.
    1 Identify a user, one success criterion and one material/time limit before building. Test a prototype against a stated criterion, change a feature and try again. Compare two choices for the task and explain a preferred choice with a demonstrated result.
    2 Distinguish what a solution must accomplish from a limit on making it; sketch a possible solution. Record before/after results and explain how a change addressed a weak part. Compare two options against a criterion and explain what is gained or given up.
    3 State a user's problem, an observable criterion and a constraint; explain why a proposed design addresses them. Run the same success test on two versions and explain an improvement using recorded results. Present a solution with a labelled model/sketch, results and one remaining weakness.
    4 Write measurable criteria and constraints with a scaffold; use them to compare possible designs before construction. Use repeated measurements to revise a prototype; explain whether the change improved the stated criterion. Compare alternatives using performance evidence and a material/cost limit; explain a tradeoff to an audience.
    5 Prioritize a user's criteria within time, materials and safety limits; justify a plan before building. Document multiple versions and comparable tests; justify a revision and distinguish improvement from an untested change. Recommend a design using more than one criterion, evidence and an acknowledged resource/access tradeoff.
    6 Define and justify a user-centered problem, measurable success criteria and resource, safety and access constraints. Conduct and document evidence-driven iterations under constraints; explain unresolved failure and a justified next test. Defend a solution using performance, cost/resources, ethics/access and tradeoffs; answer a critical question with evidence.

    Evidence examples: Individual demonstration of a changed part (K-1); before/after record and reason (2-3); criteria sheet, test record and defended recommendation (4-6). A failed prototype can provide strong learning evidence. Failure during engineering is information, not failure of the student.

    Art: observation, purposeful design and communication¶

    End of grade CST-A1: observational sketches/models CST-A2: purposeful pattern/form/design CST-A3: artistic communication/critique/revision
    K Draw or construct a representation with a noticed detail and identify what it shows. Create a repeated pattern or selected shape/form and describe one choice. Explain what an artwork/model communicates and respond to one respectful suggestion.
    1 Represent important parts observed in an object and explain their placement. Choose pattern, color or form for a stated purpose rather than adding it without a reason. Describe an intended idea and make a change after a specific helpful comment.
    2 Make a labelled sketch/model that another person can use to identify parts or relationships. Use shape, pattern or composition to guide attention or support a function; explain the choice. Explain how a visual feature communicates an idea; give specific kind feedback and show a revision.
    3 Use observed detail and relative sizes in a drawing/model to clarify a relationship. Compare two arrangements/forms and choose one for meaning, appearance or function with a reason. Present an intended message, identify a feature that supports it and revise using audience feedback.
    4 Use labelled views or an annotated model to explain observed structure while distinguishing imagined features. Select visual features for purpose/audience, test alternatives and explain how the choice affects meaning or use. Give criterion-based feedback and revise a work to clarify meaning while explaining the decision.
    5 Represent an observed system with purposeful views, relative scale and annotations; identify a representation limit. Compare form, pattern or composition alternatives for function and aesthetic intent; justify a purposeful choice. Compare interpretations, critique specific choices respectfully and document a revision consistent with intent.
    6 Produce an accurate observational representation suited to an audience; distinguish measured, estimated and symbolic features. Defend pattern/form/design choices considering aesthetic intent, function, audience and constraints. Communicate a complex idea artistically, use respectful evidence-based critique and explain how revision strengthened or changed intent.

    Evidence examples: Observed-detail explanation (K-1); annotated sketch and reason for composition (2-3); alternative designs, critique and explained revision (4-6). Do not grade realism or neatness unless explicitly taught and relevant. This visual/design strand does not certify complete arts coverage.

    Mathematics: quantities, representation and reasoning¶

    Targets use the mathematics already taught in the primary classroom. Numbers, units and operations are chosen accordingly; advanced content must not become a hidden prerequisite. A scaffold is instruction, not proof of mastery of an untaught operation.

    End of grade CST-M1: count/compare/measure CST-M2: data/pattern representation CST-M3: grade-appropriate quantities/operations/reasoning
    K Count a small set with one-to-one matching and compare length, amount or number directly. Sort objects, show a simple repeating pattern or object graph, and describe a visible comparison. Model joining/separating small quantities or compare shapes/amounts; show how the answer fits the task.
    1 Count needed materials, compare quantities and measure a length with equal nonstandard units without gaps. Make a tally/picture display or represent a repeat and explain what a symbol/unit stands for. Use taught addition/subtraction with objects or drawings to solve a material/count problem and explain the strategy.
    2 Estimate and measure a task quantity with a taught standard unit/tool; record the unit. Create a labelled table/picture/bar display of simple data and describe a comparison or pattern. Use taught quantities/operations in a practical problem; explain the strategy and check with a model or estimate.
    3 Choose an appropriate taught unit, measure consistently and compare repeated measurements. Organize repeated-test data in a labelled table/graph and describe how a result changes. Use taught equal-group/measurement reasoning to solve and check a project problem, stating what quantities mean.
    4 Measure with appropriate precision, record units and explain why a tool/unit suits the question. Choose a suitable display, label its scale/units and use it to support a comparison without distorting the data. Apply taught whole-number or fraction/measurement reasoning to a project problem and explain why the result is reasonable.
    5 Estimate, repeat measurements and explain variation/rounding appropriate to the task and tool. Represent data clearly, identify an unusual value and distinguish a pattern from a claim the data cannot support. Use taught fraction/decimal or multi-step reasoning for materials, measurement or cost; check the answer and units.
    6 Choose units/tools and a justified measurement method; explain precision, repeated results and limits. Choose and defend a data/pattern representation, interpret variation and identify a limitation or misleading presentation. Solve and defend a taught multi-step quantities problem, using ratio/rate reasoning only after prerequisites; check units and reasonableness.

    Evidence examples: Count/compare demonstration (K-1); measurement record and explained representation (2-3); checked practical calculation and interpretation of data (4-6). A graph in a group poster is not proof that every student can interpret it.

    Separate planned instructional-stage matrix¶

    This matrix is a local intended progression, not an audited lesson-coverage map or a student-results record. Every code recurs every year.

    • I - Introduced: model and encounter the foundation; collect a formative check.
    • D - Developing: guided practice with increasing independence.
    • R - Reinforced: revisit and transfer prior learning at the new grade target; continue formative checks. It does not mean the skill is finished.
    • M - Expected mastery checkpoint: plan individual assessment of that grade's target. M never means a student has mastered it merely by exposure.

    Turning a plan into verified learning¶

    1. Select a small number of primary codes for each lesson; do not claim all eighteen because students built something.
    2. Give each participating grade its own target and success criterion. An older student's answer or a mixed-grade team's product does not establish younger students' competence.
    3. Rotate meaningful roles and include a brief individual demonstration or explanation. Track who supplied which evidence, not just team completion.
    4. At M checkpoints, apply the individual mastery rule: two dated occasions, including an individual performance and a changed context or follow-up explanation, both meeting the grade target. Record missing evidence as not yet assessed, not mastered.
    5. Map actual lessons and evidence separately for weekly/bi-weekly schedules and A/B rotations. A second rotation may revisit a code with a harder task; it must not defer a grade's essential target until the student has left it.
    6. If time, weather, devices or materials change, preserve the target and valid assessment, not necessarily the activity. Use indoor alternatives, unplugged representations or another meeting when they retain the assessed skill. Do not relabel a materially different skill as equivalent.
    7. Flag unmet targets and uncertain external mappings for parent integration. This plan alone does not prove that any existing schedule can yet deliver and assess all targets within its available meetings.

    Two to four primary codes per lesson is a useful planning limit, not a mandated quota. Short checks can overlap when each skill is actually demonstrated. Keep evidence in the school-approved assessment system; do not collect personal or family data merely to make a project feel authentic.
