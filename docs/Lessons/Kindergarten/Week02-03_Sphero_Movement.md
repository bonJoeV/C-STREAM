# Weeks 2-3: Exploring Movement with Sphero

## Lesson at a glance

| Field | Plan |
|---|---|
| Grade / unit / title | K / Movement and instructions / Exploring Movement with Sphero |
| Time | Two meetings, each 25 minutes, including cleanup |
| Meaningful domains | C, T, M |
| Big idea | A tool responds to input; an algorithm is an ordered set of steps. |
| Student objective | "I can predict a two-step path, try it, and fix one step." |
| Why | Clear directions and checking results help us use tools responsibly. |
| Catholic connection | Include every partner in making decisions; tools should benefit people, not exclude them. [CCC 2405](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P8A.HTM), teacher paraphrase. |
| Local standards | CST-C2, CST-T1, CST-T2, CST-M1; local program codes, never official benchmarks. Official alignment: VERIFICATION REQUIRED. |
| Technology requirement | Optional: picture-grid pathway is primary; real Sphero driving extends tool input/output. |
| Difficulty / prep / cleanup | Beginner / Light: 10 minutes for paper kits; optional robot setup 20 minutes plus charging / 3 minutes inside each meeting |

## Before class

1. Make 5/7/10/12 teams for 10/15/20/25 students: pairs, with a final trio for odd enrollment. Prepare 5/8/10/13 kits; the extra kit at 15/25 is a reserve. Assign chooser, mover, and checker; swap roles after each trial.
2. Draw one 3-by-3 grid per pair, with 10 cm squares. Mark the lower-left square START and the upper-left square FINISH. Draw another target one square right of START. No printing is necessary.
3. Make four large cards per pair: two upward arrows, one right arrow, one STOP hand. Here arrows mean one square **up the page/right on the page**, not a robot's facing direction.
4. Draw a paper robot at least 8 cm wide per pair. Place kits at seated tables and write a roster with columns A (algorithm), I (input/output), H (real hardware drive), P (real programming).
5. Optional hardware: use one school-approved, charged Sphero and one paired tablet. Before children arrive, open the installed Sphero Edu version, select the robot, enter Drive, aim/calibrate toward the far end, and test its lowest practical speed and stop control. Keep a 1 m clear demonstration lane and an adult beside it. If pairing or safe stopping fails, omit hardware. A substitute need not troubleshoot Bluetooth during class.

## Vocabulary and teacher background

- **Input:** what we give a tool; point to an arrow or control.
- **Output:** what the tool does; move the paper robot.
- **Algorithm:** steps in order; point left to right along cards.
- **Debug:** find a mismatch, change a step, try again.

A paper model follows the stated grid rule because a person moves it. In Drive mode an adult steers continuously; this is **operating hardware, not writing a program**. Real robots can drift, lose connection, or respond to sensors; they do not necessarily execute a command exactly.

**Misconception:** "Robots know what I want." Explain that our model needs explicit steps; a machine depends on controls, software, sensors, and conditions.

**If asked, "Can robots think?"** "Some machines use complicated software to choose actions. This classroom robot responds to its controls. It is not a person."

## SAFETY

Keep children seated outside the optional robot lane. No chasing, racing, throwing, body maze, beanbag toss, or robot on a table edge. Stop the robot before retrieving it. Adult handles charging and battery compartments; inspect for damage. Use large paper pieces, not coins or tiny tokens. Offer seated pointing instead of body movement. Never physically guide a child's hands without permission.

## Meeting 1: input and output (25 minutes)

1. **0-3 (3 min):** Invite a brief prayer for including others. Show two arrow choices: "Which way will our robot move?"
2. **3-7 (4 min):** Model one arrow, one-square output, and STOP. Optional adult Drive demonstration replaces the last two minutes, not adds time. Name the difference between a card model and a real steering control.
3. **7-16 (9 min):** Pairs try UP then STOP and RIGHT then STOP from the same START. Swap roles every trial. Each child chooses and predicts one move before a partner executes it. Teacher begins individual I checks while circulating.
4. **16-20 (4 min):** Finish remaining I checks: child points to input and predicted/observed output. Accept pointing, speech, or drawing; do not repeat already documented checks.
5. **20-22 (2 min):** Ask "What did we give it? What did it do? How did we include our partner?"
6. **22-25 (3 min):** Collect cards, park paper robots, return crayons; adult stops and stores optional hardware.

## Meeting 2: sequence and debugging (25 minutes)

1. **0-3 (3 min):** Recall input/output; show START and FINISH two squares up.
2. **3-6 (3 min):** Model UP, RIGHT: it misses FINISH. Ask "Which step needs changing?" Change RIGHT to UP; reset and retest.
3. **6-15 (9 min):** Pairs build UP, UP, STOP. Mover follows cards only. Each child then tries the intentionally wrong UP, RIGHT path and changes one card. Third child receives a full chooser turn, not just checker duty. Teacher starts individual A/count checks now.
4. **15-20 (5 min):** Finish remaining individual checks while partners rehearse: each child orders two arrows, predicts the endpoint, and corrects one wrong card. Record A and count two moves aloud/on fingers; no 25-child serial exit line.
5. **20-22 (2 min):** Share one correction and one fair-turn action.
6. **22-25 (3 min):** Reassemble four-card kits, collect grids and individual drawings, clear tables.

## Evidence and what success looks like

Record I and A separately as independent / prompted / not yet. Success is a correct prediction of two one-square moves and a correction that reaches the pictured target, plus identifying a tool input/output. Neat writing, knowing left/right on one's body, or driving speed are not assessed.

H may be recorded only for a child who actually controls the real robot and stops it safely; an adult demonstration is observation, not child operation. P stays **not assessed** in this unit. No-device success demonstrates algorithms and model input/output, **not operating or programming real hardware**.

## Questions, support, and troubleshooting

- "What happens if we change the order? Did the output match your prediction?"
- **Support:** offer two pictured choices; cover unused squares; child points while adult moves the token. Avoid screen or motor demands.
- **Challenge:** design a three-command route and explain one correction; no speed competition.
- **Wrong route:** reset to the same START, read one card at a time, and fix only the first mismatch.
- **Confused arrows:** hold the page in one fixed orientation and point to the destination square.
- **Robot drift/disconnection:** adult stops, checks aim once, then returns to paper; do not report a paper success as hardware success.
- **Minnesota indoor fallback:** the seated grid is the full lesson in any weather. No outdoor or internet requirement.

## Optional family snippet

We predicted and corrected picture instructions and practiced fair turns. Ask: "Show me two steps and one way to fix them." Real robot driving was optional and was recorded separately from algorithm learning. No homework or device purchase.
